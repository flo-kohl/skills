# Why Feynman × Blurting works

Background for when a student asks "why are we doing it this way?" Explain it in plain words; the references are here if they want to dig deeper.

## Blurting is retrieval practice

Writing down everything you remember from a blank page is free recall, the hardest form of retrieval practice. Pulling knowledge out of memory strengthens it far more than putting it in again by rereading or highlighting. This is the testing effect: in classic experiments, students who practiced recalling a text remembered much more a week later than students who spent the same time rereading it, even though the rereaders felt more confident.

- Roediger & Karpicke (2006). Test-enhanced learning. *Psychological Science*.
- Karpicke & Blunt (2011). Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping. *Science*.
- Dunlosky et al. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*. Rates practice testing and distributed practice as the two most useful techniques of the ten reviewed; highlighting and rereading score low.

That's why the skill gives nothing away before the blurt: any preview turns recall into recognition, which feels easier and teaches much less.

## The gaps show you what you only thought you knew

People routinely overestimate how well they understand things until they have to explain them step by step (the illusion of explanatory depth). A blurt makes the gaps visible, and checking it against the material turns a vague feeling of "I sort of know this" into a concrete list.

- Rozenblit & Keil (2002). The misunderstood limits of folk science: an illusion of explanatory depth. *Cognitive Science*.

## Confident mistakes are the best ones to fix

Errors that people were confident about, once corrected, are remembered especially well. That's the hypercorrection effect, and it's why the check flags mistakes the student didn't mark as uncertain.

- Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001). Errors committed with high confidence are hypercorrected. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*.

## Trying before being told helps

Attempting an answer before getting the explanation, even a wrong guess, improves how well the explanation is learned. That's why the Feynman loop asks "what do you think it is?" first.

- Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009). The pretesting effect: do unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance learning? *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*.

## Explaining it yourself is the Feynman technique

The Feynman technique is simple: explain the idea in plain language, notice where the explanation breaks down, go back to the source, and simplify again. Research on self-explanation backs this up: learners who explain material to themselves understand it more deeply than those who just read it. Producing the explanation yourself also beats reading the same words (the generation effect).

- Chi et al. (1994). Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding. *Cognitive Science*.
- Slamecka & Graf (1978). The generation effect: delineation of a phenomenon. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory*.

## Spacing the rounds beats cramming

Reviewing the same material in several sessions spread over days leads to much better long-term retention than the same total time in one block. That's why the gap sheet ends with a next-blurt date and the plan of tomorrow, in 3 days, in a week.

- Cepeda et al. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: a review and quantitative synthesis. *Psychological Bulletin*.
